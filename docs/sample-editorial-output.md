# Sample editorial output (illustrative draft)

## The AI workflow is only as good as its evidence

AI tools can help teams move faster, but speed is not the same as confidence. A system may summarize a long report in seconds and still miss a qualification, repeat an outdated claim, or make an inference sound like a confirmed fact. For a newsletter editor, the hard part is not producing text. It is deciding what the evidence actually supports.

A practical editorial workflow starts with multiple sources. Read the original pages, record the claims they directly support, and note where their accounts differ. A source-linked research brief makes this work easier because the editor can move from a summary back to the article that supports it. When a page cannot be accessed, the tool should say so rather than quietly filling the gap.

The next step is to separate reporting from interpretation. “The company announced a new tool” is a factual claim only when a reliable source documents the announcement. “This could lower the cost of experimentation for small teams” is analysis: plausible, perhaps useful, but still an interpretation that should be presented as such. Keeping those categories distinct helps readers understand what is known and what remains uncertain.

Writing assistance has a similar responsibility. A long sentence may hide two separate ideas. A filler phrase may delay the point. Passive voice may obscure who acted. These patterns are useful signals, not automatic errors. Passive voice can be the right choice when the actor is unknown or unimportant, and a short sentence can still be misleading. Editors should be able to accept or reject each rewrite and compare it with the original.

The strongest use of AI is therefore not to remove human judgment. It is to make the judgment process more visible. A good workspace links claims to evidence, labels uncertainty, explains writing suggestions, and preserves the writer’s original words until a change is approved.

**Practical takeaway:** use AI to organize the work, not to outsource responsibility. Verify important claims, preserve source links, and treat every rewrite as a proposal—not a command.
